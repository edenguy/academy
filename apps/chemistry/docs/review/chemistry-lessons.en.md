## Lesson 1 — Ionic bond: a metal hands an electron over

*Charges appear, then a lattice — and the solid does not conduct*



A metal atom and a nonmetal atom do not share. The metal **gives electrons away**, and the nonmetal **takes them**. What is left is a positive ion and a negative ion. They attract. That attraction is the ionic bond.

The lesson builds only two formulas, from a fixed charge table. Sodium loses one electron and chlorine gains it: `NaCl`. Magnesium loses two, so two chlorines are needed: `MgCl2`. The charges in the formula add up to zero.

In the **solid**, the ions sit in a lattice and cannot travel, so the solid does not conduct. **Melt** it, or **dissolve** it, and the ions are free to move. Then it conducts.



> 🖩 *Interactive explorer* — Drag the electron across. Switch the pair, then the state.



**Key points**

- A positive ion and a negative ion attract. That attraction is the ionic bond, and the ions pack into a lattice.

- `NaCl` needs one electron moved. `MgCl2` needs two, so the charges still cancel.

- An ionic solid does not conduct. The melt and the solution do, because the ions can move.



**Practice questions (with correct answers and explanations, for review)**

1. **Solid sodium chloride is a lattice of ions. Does that solid conduct electricity?**
   - (a) No. The ions are stuck in place. ✅
   - (b) Yes. Any substance made of ions conducts, even as a solid.
   - (c) Yes, but only if the crystal is cold.
   - _Explanation:_ Conduction here needs ions that can travel. In the solid they cannot. Melt or dissolve the lattice and they can.

2. **Magnesium loses two electrons. Chlorine gains one. Which formula balances?**
   - (a) `MgCl`
   - (b) `MgCl2` ✅
   - (c) `Mg2Cl`
   - _Explanation:_ One `Mg^2+` needs two `Cl^-` so the charges add to zero. That is `MgCl2`.

## Lesson 2 — Covalent bond: a shared pair

*One pair, two, or three — and a lattice where nothing is a separate molecule*



Two atoms can **share** a pair of electrons instead of handing one over. That shared pair is a covalent bond. One pair is a **single** bond. Two pairs are a **double** bond. Three pairs are a **triple** bond.

A Lewis picture is just those pairs, drawn between the atoms. The count on the screen is the bond order: 1, 2, or 3 shared pairs. Nothing else is being measured.

In a **molecular** substance the sharing stops at the edge of each molecule. In a **covalent network**, like diamond, every atom is tied to its neighbors. There is no separate molecule to pick up.



> 🖩 *Interactive explorer* — Move the handle to add shared pairs. Then switch to a network.



**Key points**

- A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons. Single, double, and triple mean one, two, and three pairs.

- A Lewis picture draws those pairs between the atoms.

- Molecular substances are separate molecules. Diamond is a covalent network: every atom is bonded to its neighbors.



**Practice questions (with correct answers and explanations, for review)**

1. **A double bond is which of these?**
   - (a) Two atoms that have swapped electrons and become ions
   - (b) Two shared pairs between the same two atoms ✅
   - (c) A bond that is twice as long as a single bond
   - _Explanation:_ Bond order here is the number of shared pairs. Double means two pairs. This lesson does not measure a length.

2. **Diamond is best described as which kind of substance?**
   - (a) A pile of small separate molecules
   - (b) An ionic lattice of C+ and C−
   - (c) A covalent network, each atom bonded to its neighbors ✅
   - _Explanation:_ In diamond the covalent bonds do not stop at a molecule. They run through the whole network.

## Lesson 3 — Polarity: the shape decides

*Two equal bond dipoles. Straight across, they cancel. Bent, they don't.*



In a covalent bond the shared pair is not always shared evenly. The atom that pulls harder carries a partial negative charge, written δ−. The other end is δ+. That uneven bond is a **polar covalent bond**. An even share is **nonpolar**.

A molecule can contain polar bonds and still be nonpolar. Drag the angle between two **equal** bond dipoles. The number on the screen is their vector sum. It is 2 when they point the same way, and 0 when the angle is 180 degrees. Zero means the molecule is nonpolar.

Carbon dioxide is the straight case: the two bond dipoles point opposite ways and cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar. Water is the bent case: they do not cancel, so the molecule is polar. This lesson does not show electronegativity numbers.



> 🖩 *Interactive explorer* — Drag the angle. Watch the net dipole fall to zero when the molecule is straight.



**Key points**

- δ+ and δ− mark an uneven share. That bond is polar covalent. An even share is nonpolar.

- Two equal bond dipoles cancel at 180°. Bend them and a net dipole remains.

- CO2 is linear and nonpolar. H2O is bent and polar. The shape, not just the bonds, decides.



**Practice questions (with correct answers and explanations, for review)**

1. **Carbon dioxide has two polar bonds in a straight line. The molecule is:**
   - (a) Nonpolar, because the two bond dipoles cancel ✅
   - (b) Polar, because every polar bond makes a polar molecule
   - (c) Ionic, because the charges are partial
   - _Explanation:_ Partial charges on a straight molecule point opposite ways. The vector sum at 180° is zero, so the molecule is nonpolar.

2. **Water is bent, not straight. What does that do to the two bond dipoles?**
   - (a) They cancel, so water is nonpolar
   - (b) They do not cancel, so the molecule is polar ✅
   - (c) The bend turns the bonds into ionic bonds
   - _Explanation:_ A bend leaves a nonzero vector sum. That net dipole is why the water molecule is polar.

## Lesson 4 — Metallic bond: a sea of electrons

*The cations can slide. The sea stays, and it can drift.*



In a metal the atoms' outer electrons are not stuck to one neighbor. They form a **sea** around a lattice of positive ions. The attraction between that sea and the ions is the metallic bond.

Drag the shear handle. The top row of cations slides over by one spacing, and all six cations are still there. The sea did not snap. That is why a metal can be **hammered and shaped** instead of cracking the way a brittle ionic crystal does.

The second handle drifts the sea. Electrons moving through the solid are why a metal **conducts as a solid**. No temperature and no conductivity number are shown — only whether the sea is drifting.



> 🖩 *Interactive explorer* — Shear the lattice, then drift the electron sea.



**Key points**

- A metal is a lattice of positive ions in a sea of electrons. That attraction is the metallic bond.

- The ions can slide past each other and the sea still holds them. That is malleability.

- The sea can drift through the solid, so a metal conducts without being melted.



**Practice questions (with correct answers and explanations, for review)**

1. **Why can a metal conduct electricity while it is still solid?**
   - (a) Its ions are free to walk through the crystal
   - (b) Electrons in the sea can drift through the solid ✅
   - (c) It has already melted on the inside
   - _Explanation:_ The cations stay in the lattice. The mobile part is the electron sea, and it can move while the metal is solid.

2. **You shear a metal lattice and it bends instead of shattering. Why?**
   - (a) The ions slide, and the electron sea still surrounds them ✅
   - (b) The bonds are ionic, so opposite charges lock the new position
   - (c) Shearing removes the electrons, so nothing is left to break
   - _Explanation:_ After the rows slide, the cations are still inside the sea. The metallic bond does not depend on one fixed neighbor.

## Lesson 5 — Between molecules, a weaker pull

*The line inside water stays solid. The link between waters is dashed.*



The bond **inside** a water molecule is covalent: each hydrogen shares a pair with the oxygen. That line stays solid no matter how you drag. Forces **between** molecules are a different thing, and they are weaker than that covalent bond.

Every molecule attracts its neighbors a little. Those weak pulls are van der Waals forces. A **hydrogen bond** is the stronger case of that family in this lesson: a hydrogen that is already bonded to oxygen reaches toward the oxygen of another water molecule. Drag the second molecule until one of its hydrogens points at the other oxygen and sits close. A dashed line appears. Turn it away, or pull it far, and the line goes.

Boiling is these between-molecule links letting go, while the covalent bonds inside each molecule stay. Hydrogen bonds are why water hangs on to its neighbors more stubbornly than a similar molecule that cannot make them. No boiling temperature is shown.



> 🖩 *Interactive explorer* — Drag the second water. A dashed line means a hydrogen bond.



**Key points**

- Inside one water molecule the O–H links are covalent. Between water molecules the pull is weaker.

- Van der Waals forces are the weak attraction between molecules. A hydrogen bond needs a hydrogen already bonded to oxygen (or nitrogen, or fluorine) pointing at such an atom on a neighbor.

- Boiling lets the links between molecules go. It does not break the covalent bonds inside them. No temperature is shown here.



**Practice questions (with correct answers and explanations, for review)**

1. **The solid line between O and H inside one water molecule is:**
   - (a) A covalent bond, a shared pair ✅
   - (b) A hydrogen bond
   - (c) A van der Waals force
   - _Explanation:_ Hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces are between molecules. Inside the molecule, O and H share a pair.

2. **When does the dashed line between two water molecules appear?**
   - (a) Whenever the two oxygens exist, at any distance
   - (b) When a hydrogen of one points at the oxygen of the other, and is close ✅
   - (c) Only after the covalent bond inside a molecule breaks
   - _Explanation:_ The dashed line is the hydrogen bond. It needs the hydrogen pointing at the neighboring oxygen, within the reach defined in the lesson's model. Pull them apart or turn the hydrogen away and it disappears.

## Lesson 6 — Four kinds of material

*Same probes. The structure picks the answer.*



The four structures in this chapter behave differently because the **particles** and the **links** are different. Ionic: a lattice of ions. Molecular: separate molecules, with weaker forces between them. Metallic: cations in an electron sea. Covalent network: atoms bonded to their neighbors all the way through, like diamond.

Pick a structure, then a probe: conduct as a solid, conduct when melted, dissolve, melt easily. The word on the screen is looked up from one table, the school model for these four. **Varies** is an answer, not a shrug. Not every ionic compound dissolves easily. Metals do not share one melting point.

The network row is **diamond**. It does not conduct. Graphite is also a covalent network, and graphite does conduct, so "covalent network" is not the same claim as "never conducts." This probe is about the diamond example.



> 🖩 *Interactive explorer* — Choose a structure and a probe. The answer comes from the table.



**Key points**

- Identify the material by its particles: ions, separate molecules, cations in an electron sea, or a covalent network.

- Ionic solids do not conduct; the melt does. Metals conduct as solids. Diamond's network does not.

- Solubility of ionic compounds, and the melting point of metals, are marked varies. Diamond is the network example, not graphite.



**Practice questions (with correct answers and explanations, for review)**

1. **An ionic solid and a metal bar. Which one conducts while it is still solid?**
   - (a) Both
   - (b) Only the metal ✅
   - (c) Only the ionic solid
   - _Explanation:_ The metal's electron sea can drift in the solid. The ionic solid's ions cannot move until it is melted or dissolved.

2. **The network probe in this lesson uses diamond. Which statement matches that example?**
   - (a) Diamond conducts, because every covalent network conducts
   - (b) Diamond does not conduct and does not dissolve; graphite is a different network and does conduct ✅
   - (c) Diamond is a molecular substance with weak forces between small molecules
   - _Explanation:_ The table's network row is diamond: no conduction, no dissolving, hard to melt. Graphite is also a covalent network, and it does conduct, so the lesson does not claim that every network behaves like diamond.
